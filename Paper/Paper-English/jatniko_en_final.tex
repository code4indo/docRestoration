% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
Historical handwritten documents suffer from various degradation artifacts that significantly impair Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR) systems. While conventional document restoration methods focus on visual quality, they often fail to preserve text readability for HTR tasks. This paper proposes an HTR-oriented document restoration framework based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) with two key contributions: (1) integration of a frozen pre-trained HTR recognizer that provides stable text-aware gradients without joint training instabilities, and (2) systematic multi-component loss function optimization through ablation studies. Unlike conventional approaches that rely solely on image-level supervision, our method explicitly optimizes text readability through gradients from the frozen recognizer. We conduct quantitative evaluation on a realistic semi-synthetic dataset constructed from authentic 16th-18th century paleographic documents from the National Archives of the Republic of Indonesia (ANRI) with controlled degradation augmentation to enable paired ground-truth evaluation. On 712 test images, our framework achieves a Character Error Rate (CER) of 34.9\%, reducing CER from 83.4\% (degraded condition) with 58.2\% relative improvement, while maintaining high visual quality (PSNR 30.74~dB, SSIM 0.987). This performance approaches the theoretical maximum on clean images (CER 34.1\%). Qualitative validation on 15 real ANRI manuscripts with authentic historical degradation demonstrates effective generalization to damage patterns that cannot be replicated synthetically. Ablation studies reveal that a 4-component loss configuration (pixel, adversarial, perceptual, CTC) achieves optimal balance, while dual-modal discriminator exploration shows marginal contribution ($\Delta$PSNR +0.28~dB, $p>0.05$), confirming that recognizer freezing strategy and loss optimization are more critical than discriminator architectural complexity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{H}{istorical} handwritten documents constitute invaluable cultural heritage, containing critical information about past events, governance, trade, and social structures. The National Archives of the Republic of Indonesia (ANRI) preserves an extensive collection of colonial-era documents from the 16th to 18th centuries, including materials recognized by UNESCO as part of the Memory of the World Register.

These documents, written in Dutch paleographic script during the VOC (Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie) and Dutch East Indies periods, provide unique insights into Indonesia's historical development. However, these manuscripts have suffered severe degradation including (Figure~\ref{fig:degradation_examples}): ink bleed-through, iron gall ink corrosion, paper aging, physical damage, and fading ink.

Such degradation significantly impairs text readability and poses substantial challenges for Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR) systems.

These degradation artifacts severely disrupt HTR system performance, which is essential for large-scale digitization, transcription, search, indexing, and information extraction from historical archives. Despite substantial advances in HTR technology using deep learning approaches, particularly Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNN) and transformer-based architectures, these systems remain highly sensitive to image quality and degradation levels.

% Figure 1: Examples of degradation types in historical documents

\subsection{Limitations of yang ada Approaches}

Degraded historical documents present unique challenges for HTR systems, as noise, stains, blur, and background degradation can significantly reduce recognition accuracy. Automated document restoration aims to enhance image quality before HTR processing, yet yang ada approaches have fundamental limitations:

First, conventional document restoration methods (including classical thresholding such as Otsu~\cite{otsu1979threshold} and Sauvola~\cite{sauvola2000adaptive}, as well as deep learning approaches based on U-Net and GAN) optimize purely visual metrics (PSNR, SSIM) without considering the impact on HTR readability. Consequently, models may produce visually appealing images while damaging text structure (e.g., broken strokes, missing character connections) that is critical for HTR accuracy.

Second, HTR integration in document restoration has been proposed by Souibgui \textit{et al.}~\cite{souibgui2021enhance} through joint training of generator and recognizer. While innovative, this approach uses a single-modal discriminator that only evaluates visual quality, and joint training can cause optimization instabilities as the generator and recognizer compete for conflicting gradients.

Third, an evaluation gap exists—most benchmarks (DIBCO series) only provide visual ground truth without text transcriptions, making HTR impact validation infeasible without additional annotations. Datasets for 16th-18th century paleographic documents with authentic degradation are limited.

This research addresses these limitations with the following contributions. Core contributions: (1) Frozen HTR recognizer strategy providing stable text-aware gradients without joint training instabilities ($p<0.001$); (2) Multi-component loss function optimization through systematic ablation studies identifying an optimal 4-component configuration (pixel, adversarial, perceptual, CTC). Methodological contributions: (3) Evaluation on authentic 16th-18th century paleographic manuscripts from the National Archives of Indonesia; (4) Ablation studies measuring each component's contribution, including the finding that dual-modal discriminator provides marginal contribution ($\Delta$PSNR +0.28~dB, $p>0.05$), confirming that training protocol and generator capacity are more critical than discriminator architectural complexity.

\section{Related Work}

This section reviews prior research in three interconnected areas: document image restoration and enhancement, generative adversarial networks for image restoration, and handwritten text recognition systems.

\subsection{Document Image Restoration and Enhancement}

Document restoration aims to recover degraded documents by removing noise, artifacts, and background degradation while preserving text readability for OCR/HTR systems or human reading. Unlike binarization which produces binary output (black-and-white), document restoration can generate grayscale or color output that retains pixel intensity information for better visual quality.

\subsubsection{Classical Methods: Binarization and Thresholding}

Early approaches to document enhancement focused on binarization using thresholding. Global binarization methods such as Otsu~\cite{otsu1979threshold} determine a single threshold value for the entire image by maximizing inter-class variance. While computationally efficient, global methods tend to be less effective on documents with varying illumination or complex degradation.

Local adaptive thresholding methods address this limitation by computing pixel-specific thresholds based on local neighborhoods. Sauvola and Pietikäinen~\cite{sauvola2000adaptive} proposed a widely-used method that combines local mean and standard deviation. Niblack~\cite{niblack1986introduction} and Wolf~\cite{wolf2004extraction} developed similar local approaches with different adaptation strategies. However, these thresholding-based methods produce binary output that loses pixel intensity information and may eliminate fine details such as thin strokes.

\subsubsection{Deep Learning Methods for Document Restoration}

Since the mid-2010s, with advances in deep learning and availability of large labeled datasets such as DIBCO~\cite{pratikakis2016hdibco}, document restoration approaches have shifted from binarization to grayscale enhancement that preserves intensity information. U-Net-based auto-encoders~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) enable end-to-end learning of mappings from degraded to clean documents with potential to preserve fine details critical for HTR.

\subsection{Generative Adversarial Networks for Image Restoration}

GANs~\cite{goodfellow2016improved} have substantially transformed the field of image restoration with their ability to generate realistic outputs through adversarial training between generator and discriminator. Unlike auto-encoders that only minimize reconstruction loss, GANs can produce sharper details and more natural textures.

\subsubsection{Conditional GANs for Image-to-Image Translation}

Pix2pix~\cite{isola2017image} introduced the conditional GAN (cGAN) framework for image-to-image translation tasks, where the generator receives an input image and produces a corresponding output image. The PatchGAN discriminator in Pix2pix evaluates authenticity at local patch level, enabling better capture of high-frequency details. This framework has been successfully applied to various domains including colorization, style transfer, and semantic segmentation.

CycleGAN~\cite{zhu2017unpaired} extends this concept to unpaired image-to-image translation using cycle consistency loss, enabling training without paired data. While useful for domain adaptation, unpaired approaches are less suitable for document restoration where paired degraded-clean data is available and pixel-level accuracy is crucial for preserving text content.

\subsubsection{GANs for Document Restoration and Enhancement}

In the context of document restoration, several GAN approaches have been proposed:

DE-GAN~\cite{souibgui2020degan} uses a U-Net generator with PatchGAN discriminator for document enhancement tasks including deblurring, watermark removal, and background cleaning. While achieving high visual quality, DE-GAN focuses on optimizing pixel-level similarity and does not explicitly integrate HTR readability metrics in its loss function.

DocEnTr~\cite{souibgui2022docent} introduces a document enhancement transformer that combines self-attention mechanisms with adversarial training. This approach demonstrates capability to capture long-range dependencies, but has high computational cost and has not integrated explicit HTR guidance.

HTR-GAN (Souibgui et al.)~\cite{souibgui2021enhance} represents the most relevant work to this research, integrating an HTR recognizer within the GAN framework for document enhancement. The generator and recognizer are jointly trained with combined losses from adversarial, reconstruction, and CTC. The joint training approach potentially causes optimization instabilities as the recognizer and generator compete for gradients—a theoretical concern that motivates the frozen recognizer approach in this research.

Distinctions of Proposed Approach: This research fundamentally differs from HTR-GAN in: (1) Frozen recognizer—we use a frozen pre-trained recognizer for stable gradients, not joint training; (2) Dual-modal discriminator—our discriminator processes images (CNN) and text sequences (LSTM) in parallel with potential for visual-textual coherence evaluation (although ablation studies show marginal impact, see Section~VI); (3) Adaptive loss balancing—adaptive balancing mechanism that dynamically adjusts ratios between CTC loss and visual losses for optimal balance; (4) Enhanced architecture—residual blocks, attention gates, and spatial attention for better preservation of thin strokes.

\subsection{Handwritten Text Recognition Systems}

HTR systems aim to recognize handwritten text from document images. The evolution of HTR has progressed from classical HMM-based methods to modern deep learning architectures.

CRNN (Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network)~\cite{shi2016crnn} combines CNN for feature extraction with RNN (typically LSTM/GRU) for sequence modeling, trained with CTC loss for alignment-free recognition. This architecture has become the foundation for many modern HTR systems due to its ability to handle variable-length sequences without explicit segmentation.

Attention mechanisms have improved HTR performance by enabling models to focus on relevant regions when recognizing each character. Encoder-decoder architectures with attention~\cite{bluche2017scan} can learn character segmentation and alignment implicitly.

Transformer-based HTR~\cite{michael2019evaluating,diaz2021rethinking} leverages self-attention to capture long-range dependencies without recurrent connections. Vision transformers and hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on various handwriting benchmarks, including historical and modern documents.

Relevance to This Research: HTR systems are sensitive to image quality—degradation such as noise, blur, or broken strokes can increase CER. This research uses a frozen pre-trained hybrid CNN-Transformer recognizer to extract recognition features that guide the generator to produce HTR-readable output. The frozen recognizer approach differs from joint training because: (1) stable gradients—recognizer weights do not change; (2) consistent evaluation—recognizer performance does not degrade during GAN training; (3) modular design—recognizer can be updated independently.

\section{Proposed Method}

This section presents the proposed HTR-oriented document restoration framework. We first provide an architectural overview, then detail each component: the U-Net generator, dual-modal discriminator, frozen HTR recognizer integration, and optimized multi-component loss function.

\subsection{Framework Overview}

To address the limitations of conventional restoration methods that ignore machine text readability, this research proposes an HTR-oriented GAN framework that explicitly integrates a text recognizer in the training loop. The proposed framework, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}, operates on degraded handwritten document line images and produces clean, readable output optimized for both visual quality and HTR performance.

The restoration process flow is as follows: A degraded image $I_{\text{deg}}$ is input to generator $G$ which produces a restored image $I_{\text{gen}}$. The dual-modal discriminator $D$ receives pairs $(I_{\text{gt}}, \text{text}_{\text{gt}})$ as real samples and $(I_{\text{gen}}, \text{text}_{\text{pred}})$ as fake samples for adversarial learning. In parallel, the frozen HTR recognizer $R$ extracts features from $I_{\text{gen}}$ to ensure text readability through CTC loss and recognition feature loss.

The framework consists of four main components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Generator (G): Enhanced U-Net architecture mapping degraded images $I_{\text{deg}}$ to restored images $I_{\text{gen}}$. The generator is equipped with Residual Dense Blocks and Attention Gates for preserving fine text details while removing background noise.
    
    \item Dual-Modal Discriminator (D): Discriminator with parallel CNN and BiLSTM pathways that assess visual quality and text coherence simultaneously. Bilateral cross-attention fusion mechanism enables visual-textual consistency validation to avoid artifacts that damage character structure.
    
    \item Frozen HTR Recognizer (R): Pre-trained Transformer-based recognizer with fixed weights providing HTR-aware gradients without catastrophic forgetting risk. The recognizer achieves CER 33.72\% on initial ANRI validation dataset ($n$=948), and CER 26.57\% (validation set, $n$=710) and 34.1\% (test set, $n$=712) on clean ground truth images in this study—differences reflect text complexity variation across dataset splits.
    
    \item Multi-Component Loss Function: Weighted combination of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}}$ ($\lambda=50$), $\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}$ ($\lambda=3$), $\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}$ ($\lambda=1$), $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec\_feat}}$ ($\lambda=8$), and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}}$ ($\lambda=0.15$) optimized through curriculum learning to balance visual quality and text readability.
\end{enumerate}

Unlike HTR-GAN~\cite{souibgui2021enhance} which jointly trains the recognizer with the generator, our method uses a frozen recognizer for training stability. The dual-modal discriminator architecture serves as exploration for bilateral visual-textual validation not present in previous methods that rely only on single visual discriminators.

The training process adopts alternating adversarial strategy with three-phase curriculum learning (detailed in Section~III-F):

\begin{itemize}
    \item Discriminator update: $D$ is trained to distinguish real pairs $(I_{\text{gt}}, \text{text}_{\text{gt}})$ from generated pairs $(I_{\text{gen}}, \text{text}_{\text{pred}})$, with dual-modal supervision on visual and textual domains simultaneously.
    
    \item Generator update: $G$ is optimized to fool $D$ while minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}}$ against ground truth, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}$ for perceptual structure preservation, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec\_feat}}$ for HTR feature consistency, and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}}$ for explicit text readability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Generator Architecture: Enhanced U-Net with Residual Blocks}

This research adopts an Enhanced U-Net architecture that integrates four techniques for high-quality document restoration. Unlike conventional U-Net that only uses standard convolutional blocks, the Enhanced architecture integrates: (1) Residual Dense Blocks (RDB)~\cite{zhang2018residual} for hierarchical feature extraction with better gradient flow, (2) Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM)~\cite{woo2018cbam} for adaptive feature recalibration on channel and spatial dimensions, (3) Multi-Scale Feature Pyramid (MSFP)~\cite{lin2017fpn} with dilated convolutions~\cite{chen2017deeplab} at the bottleneck for multi-resolution context aggregation, and (4) Attention Gates~\cite{oktay2018attention} in the decoder for selective focus on text-bearing regions while suppressing background noise amplification. This combination of techniques enables preservation of thin strokes and paleographic diacritics while removing complex degradations such as bleed-through, stains, and noise.

\subsubsection{Network Specification}

Table~\ref{tab:generator_arch} details the complete generator architecture. The encoder progressively downsamples the input image through five stages, extracting multi-scale features at resolutions from $1024 \times 128$ to $32 \times 4$ (format: width $\times$ height). Each encoder block consists of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Residual Dense Block (RDB): Three densely-connected convolutional layers (kernel $3 \times 3$) with growth rate 32, producing rich features through local fusion from all preceding layers. Unlike standard ResNet~\cite{he2016deep} which has only one shortcut connection, RDB uses dense connections allowing each layer to access features from all previous layers, enhancing representation capacity and gradient flow.
    \item Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM): Sequential attention mechanism consisting of: (a) channel attention with reduction ratio 8, using shared MLP for global average pooling and global max pooling pathways, and (b) spatial attention with kernel $7 \times 7$, combining channel-wise average and max pooling to generate focused attention maps. CBAM enables the network to adaptively focus on informative feature channels and relevant spatial regions.
    \item Batch normalization (momentum 0.9, eps $10^{-5}$) and LeakyReLU activation ($\alpha=0.2$)
    \item Residual connections for effective gradient propagation in deep architectures
\end{itemize}

The bottleneck at resolution $4 \times 32$ (height $\times$ width) uses Multi-Scale Feature Pyramid (MSFP)~\cite{lin2017fpn} with three dilated convolution scales (dilation rates 1, 2, 4)~\cite{chen2017deeplab} to capture multi-resolution context, followed by 512-channel RDB and CBAM for global feature refinement. The decoder mirrors the encoder with five upsampling stages, each equipped with attention gates for text region focus:

\begin{equation}
\alpha = \sigma(W_g^T g + W_x^T x + b)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $g \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$ is the decoder feature from coarser scale (gating signal), $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$ is the skip connection feature from the encoder, $W_g$ and $W_x$ are learnable projection matrices, $b$ is bias, and $\sigma$ is sigmoid activation function. The attention coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]^{H \times W \times C}$ is then element-wise multiplied with the skip connection $x$ to suppress irrelevant background features while enhancing text-bearing regions: $\hat{x} = \alpha \odot x$. This mechanism allows the decoder to adaptively select relevant high-resolution features from the encoder.

\subsubsection{Design Rationale}

Residual Dense Blocks provide dual advantages: (1) better gradient propagation through multi-path dense connections, enabling training of deep architectures without gradient degradation, and (2) hierarchical feature reuse where each layer can access features from all previous layers, enhancing representation capacity without substantially increasing parameters. CBAM provides adaptive selectivity on channel and spatial dimensions, allowing the network to automatically focus on informative feature channels ("what") and relevant spatial regions ("where"), effective for suppressing background noise while enhancing text stroke features. Multi-Scale Feature Pyramid~\cite{lin2017fpn} at the bottleneck captures multi-resolution context through dilated convolutions~\cite{chen2017deeplab} with different scales, important for handling degradations of varying sizes (point noise vs. large stains). Attention Gates~\cite{oktay2018attention} provide spatial selectivity on skip connections, allowing the decoder to focus on text-bearing regions while suppressing noise amplification—crucial for preserving thin strokes and paleographic diacritics. The combination of these techniques yields an architecture capable of preserving fine details while removing complex degradations.

\caption{Enhanced Dual-Modal Discriminator Architecture with bilateral cross-modal attention: CNN branch (512-D) with spatial attention, BiLSTM branch (512-D) with self-attention. Bilateral fusion (128-D) enables holistic validation.}
\label{fig:discriminator_architecture}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:discriminator_architecture} visualizes the dual-modal architecture that distinguishes this approach from conventional discriminators. Unlike PatchGAN~\cite{isola2017image} which evaluates only visual patches, this architecture combines spatial analysis (CNN) and sequential analysis (BiLSTM) for holistic validation. The key characteristic is bilateral cross-modal attention (Img$\leftrightarrow$Text) enabling bidirectional interaction: the image branch validates text coherence from the HTR recognizer, while the text branch verifies visual stroke quality. This bilateral mechanism provides richer gradient feedback to the generator with 52\% parameter reduction (17.4M vs 137M baseline), driving simultaneous improvements in visual quality and textual consistency.

Image Branch (CNN with Spatial Attention): This branch extracts spatial visual features through a ResNet-based architecture. Input image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 1024 \times 1}$ is processed through an initial convolutional layer (64 channels) with batch normalization and LeakyReLU activation ($\alpha=0.2$). Four residual downsampling blocks progressively reduce spatial resolution while increasing channel depth:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
128 \times 1024 \times 64 &\rightarrow 64 \times 512 \times 64 \rightarrow 32 \times 256 \times 128 \\
&\rightarrow 16 \times 128 \times 256 \rightarrow 8 \times 64 \times 512
\end{split}
\end{equation}
A spatial attention mechanism with $3 \times 3$ kernel is applied to the 512-channel features to focus on text-bearing regions:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\text{spatial}} = \sigma(\text{Conv}_{3 \times 3}(F_{\text{img}}))
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function and $F_{\text{img}}$ is the feature map with dimensions $8 \times 64 \times 512$. Following spatial attention, an additional residual block with 512 channels (ResBlock-512) performs further feature processing without altering spatial dimensions, then global average pooling aggregates the $8 \times 64 \times 512$ spatial features into a representation vector $F_{\text{img}}^{\text{global}} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$.

Text Branch (BiLSTM with Self-Attention): This branch captures sequential text coherence. Input character sequence $S \in \mathbb{N}^{L}$ (length $L \leq 128$) is first projected to embedding space $E \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 128}$. BiLSTM processes the sequence bidirectionally to capture contextual dependencies:

\begin{equation}
h_t = [\overrightarrow{h_t}; \overleftarrow{h_t}] = [\text{LSTM}_{\text{fwd}}(E_t); \text{LSTM}_{\text{bwd}}(E_t)]
\end{equation}

\noindent producing hidden states $H \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 512}$. Multi-head scaled dot-product self-attention is applied:

\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Q, K, V$ are linear projections of $H$. Global average pooling yields text vector $F_{\text{text}}^{\text{global}} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$.

Cross-Modal Fusion: Both feature vectors $F_{\text{img}}^{\text{global}} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$ and $F_{\text{text}}^{\text{global}} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$ are processed through a bilateral cross-modal attention mechanism to enable bidirectional interaction between modalities. The bilateral approach is necessary because: (1) images need to validate text coherence predicted by the HTR recognizer, and (2) text needs to verify the visual stroke quality that generates those characters. Thus, both modalities mutually provide context for more robust anomaly detection. Each modality projects its features to a low-dimensional shared space $d=128$ (reduced from 256 for stability) through six separate projection matrices:

Image$\rightarrow$Text Attention: Image as query, text as key/value:
\begin{align}
Q_{\text{img}} &= W_{\text{img}}^Q F_{\text{img}}^{\text{global}} \nonumber\\
K_{\text{text}} &= W_{\text{text}}^K F_{\text{text}}^{\text{global}} \nonumber\\
V_{\text{text}} &= W_{\text{text}}^V F_{\text{text}}^{\text{global}} \label{eq:cross_img_proj}\\
F_{\text{img}}^{\text{att}} &= \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_{\text{img}} K_{\text{text}}^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V_{\text{text}} \label{eq:cross_img_att}
\end{align}

\noindent where $W_{\text{img}}^Q, W_{\text{text}}^K, W_{\text{text}}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 128}$ are learned projection matrices.

Text$\rightarrow$Image Attention: Text as query, image as key/value:
\begin{align}
Q_{\text{text}} &= W_{\text{text}}^Q F_{\text{text}}^{\text{global}} \nonumber\\
K_{\text{img}} &= W_{\text{img}}^K F_{\text{img}}^{\text{global}} \nonumber\\
V_{\text{img}} &= W_{\text{img}}^V F_{\text{img}}^{\text{global}} \label{eq:cross_text_proj}\\
F_{\text{text}}^{\text{att}} &= \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_{\text{text}} K_{\text{img}}^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V_{\text{img}} \label{eq:cross_text_att}
\end{align}

\noindent where $W_{\text{text}}^Q, W_{\text{img}}^K, W_{\text{img}}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 128}$ are symmetric projection matrices

Both bilaterally-attended features ($F_{\text{img}}^{\text{att}}, F_{\text{text}}^{\text{att}} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$) are concatenated into a fused vector: $F_{\text{fused}} = [F_{\text{img}}^{\text{att}}; F_{\text{text}}^{\text{att}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$. The classification head consists of two dense layers (512 $\rightarrow$ 256 neurons) with dropout and LeakyReLU activation, followed by an output layer with sigmoid activation producing validity score $D(I, S) \in [0, 1]$ to distinguish genuine image-text pairs from generator-produced fake pairs.

Hyperparameter Configuration: Spatial attention kernel uses $3 \times 3$ size (reduced from $7 \times 7$ to minimize white-dot artifacts on strokes); cross-modal projection dimension set to 128 (reduced from 256 to improve training stability); LSTM units per direction are 256, yielding total BiLSTM output of 512 dimensions. Batch normalization momentum is 0.9 (increased from 0.8 for smoother gradient flow); dropout rate is 0.1 on classification head (reduced from 0.3 to preserve thin stroke information) and 0.2 on text embedding; activation function is LeakyReLU ($\alpha=0.2$) for CNN and sigmoid for LSTM recurrent activation (ensuring compatibility with standard LSTM implementations without cuDNN).

\subsubsection{Design Rationale}

Dual-Modal Approach: Text documents possess dual characteristics: (1) visual appearance (stroke quality, noise level), (2) sequential structure (character flow, inter-word spacing). The CNN pathway captures spatial quality, while BiLSTM captures sequential coherence. Bilateral cross-modal fusion enables the discriminator to assess image-text pairs holistically with bidirectional interaction, providing more informative adversarial signals to the generator for simultaneous improvements in visual quality and textual consistency.

Parameter Efficiency: The proposed discriminator achieves significant parameter efficiency (17.4M, 52\% reduction from 137M baseline) through simplified architecture without sacrificing discriminative capability: (1) low-dimensional projection (128) for cross-modal fusion reduces computational complexity while maintaining representation capacity; (2) smaller spatial attention kernel (3$\times$3) focuses on local details without artifacts; (3) optimized residual blocks balance architectural depth with training stability. Higher batch normalization momentum (0.9) yields more stable statistics and smoother gradient flow; lower dropout rate (0.1) preserves thin stroke information crucial for degraded document discrimination.

Capabilities: Through the combination of CNN spatial analysis and BiLSTM sequence processing, the discriminator can detect:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Broken or interrupted strokes disrupting text flow (via BiLSTM sequence anomaly detection)
    \item Noise patterns, artifacts, and bleed-through (via CNN spatial features)
    \item Inconsistent stroke width (via spatial attention mechanism)
    \item Unnatural gaps or connections between characters (via cross-modal fusion analysis)
\end{itemize}

By combining spatial and sequential assessment, the discriminator provides richer feedback to the generator, driving production of images that are both visually clean and textually coherent.

\subsection{Frozen HTR Recognizer Integration}

To explicitly optimize HTR performance, this research integrates a pretrained text recognizer with weight freezing strategy, where the recognizer is frozen (\texttt{model.trainable=False}) and used as a feature extractor.

\subsubsection{Architecture and Training Configuration}

The hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture employed consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item CNN Backbone: 4 convolutional blocks with progressive feature extraction, BatchNormalization, and GELU activation
    \item Sequence Projection: Dense layer + LayerNormalization + Dropout
    \item Transformer Encoder: 6 layers, 8 attention heads, positional encoding
    \item Output: CTC decoder for sequence prediction
\end{itemize}

The recognizer was trained on 4,739 Dutch handwritten line images from 16th--18th century ANRI colonial-era documents with 80/20 split (training $n$=3,791, validation $n$=948), achieving CER performance of 33.72\% on validation set. The model was then frozen (\texttt{trainable=False}) and integrated into the GAN training pipeline.

While CER 33.72\% is moderate, this performance is comparable to state-of-the-art on historical paleographic datasets (READ 2016: 25--35\%~\cite{sanchez2016icfhr}, ICDAR 2017: 28--42\%~\cite{sanchez2017icdar}) and reflects the intrinsic complexity of 16th--18th century handwriting. The HTR component functions as a feature extractor for text-aware guidance, not a visual optimizer.

Ablation study (Section~V-B, Table~\ref{table_ablation_loss}) validates the HTR role: at 15-epoch training, configuration with HTR (Experiment~4) achieved CER 29.66\%, while baseline without HTR (Experiment~1) could not measure CER as recognizer was not integrated. Degraded input showed CER 83.4\%. Production model with extended training (50 epochs) and HTR integration achieved CER 27.11\% on validation set ($n$=710) and CER 34.9\% on test set ($n$=712) with PSNR 30.74~dB. Note: The 7.79 percentage point CER difference between validation and test is not an overfitting indicator, but reflects intrinsic text complexity differences across splits\textemdash frozen recognizer on clean ground truth images also shows similar difference (26.57\% validation vs 34.1\% test, $\Delta$=7.53\%).

The primary HTR role is ensuring text preservation and readability (CER improvement 48.5--56.3 absolute points from degraded input on test and validation sets), while PSNR improvement stems from combined HTR guidance, adversarial training, perceptual loss, and extended training. This approach demonstrates method robustness to realistic HTR components without requiring perfect recognizer.

\subsubsection{Benefits of Freezing Strategy}

The recognizer freezing approach provides implementation advantages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Training stability: No joint optimization or conflicting gradients between generator and recognizer
    \item Computational efficiency: No backpropagation through recognizer, reducing computational burden
    \item Evaluation consistency: Fixed weights provide consistent CER/WER metrics throughout training
    \item Labeled supervision: Uses ground truth text for CTC loss supervision
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Feature Extraction and Loss Computation}

This research extracts intermediate recognition features from the frozen recognizer:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I) = R_{\text{encoder}}(I)
\end{equation}

where $R_{\text{encoder}}$ represents the frozen encoder portion of the recognizer. Specifically, we use output from the projection layer before the transformer encoder, yielding feature maps with dimensions (batch, 128, 512). This layer was selected because it contains sequence-level features before contextual encoding, providing stable gradients for the generator. These features capture text-relevant characteristics such as stroke patterns, character shapes, and spatial arrangements.

Recognition feature loss between generated and ground truth images is computed using Mean Squared Error (MSE):

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec-feat}} &= \text{MSE}(\mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I_{\text{gen}}), \mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I_{\text{gt}})) \\
&= \|\mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I_{\text{gen}}) - \mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I_{\text{gt}})\|_2^2
\end{split}
\end{equation}

MSE was chosen because it is more sensitive to large feature deviations, providing stronger gradients for feature alignment improvement. This drives the generator to produce images whose text features match those of clean ground truth, implicitly enhancing HTR readability.

\subsection{Multi-Component Loss Function}

The generator is trained with a combination of five loss components calibrated through empirical grid search and validated on actual implementation:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = &\lambda_{\text{adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} + \lambda_{\text{pixel}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}} + \lambda_{\text{perc}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}} \\
&+ \lambda_{\text{ctc}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ctc}} + \lambda_{\text{rec-feat}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec-feat}}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Loss weight configuration for each component is shown in Table~\ref{table_optimal_weights}.

\subsubsection{Adversarial Loss}

Standard GAN adversarial loss drives realistic image generation:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} = -\log D(I_{\text{deg}}, I_{\text{gen}})
\end{equation}

Discriminator is trained to maximize:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_D = -[&\log D(I_{\text{deg}}, I_{\text{gt}}) \\
&+ \log(1 - D(I_{\text{deg}}, I_{\text{gen}}))]
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pixel-Level Reconstruction Loss}

L1 loss ensures pixel-by-pixel similarity with ground truth:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}} = \|I_{\text{gen}} - I_{\text{gt}}\|_1
\end{equation}

L1 norm is chosen because it produces sharper results compared to L2 norm and is more robust to outliers.

\subsubsection{Perceptual Loss}

Perceptual loss~\cite{johnson2016perceptual} compares high-level features from pretrained VGG network:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}} = \sum_{l \in L} \|\phi_l(I_{\text{gen}}) - \phi_l(I_{\text{gt}})\|_2
\end{equation}

where $\phi_l$ denotes features from layer $l$ of VGG-19 pretrained on ImageNet. Features are extracted from multiple convolutional layers for multiscale perceptual comparison. This approach ensures preservation of visual characteristics from low-level details (edges, textures) to high-level semantic features (stroke patterns, character shapes).

\subsubsection{Recognition Feature Loss}

As described in Section III-D, this component drives HTR-friendly characteristics using Mean Squared Error:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec-feat}} = \|\mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I_{\text{gen}}) - \mathcal{F}_{\text{rec}}(I_{\text{gt}})\|_2^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{Training Strategy}

\subsubsection{Curriculum Learning and Optimization Protocol}

Training employs a three-phase curriculum learning approach for optimal stability and gradual convergence:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Visual Warmup Phase (epochs 1--10): Exclusive training on visual quality without text supervision, allowing generator to build basic reconstruction capability. This phase prevents early mode collapse that can occur if CTC loss is introduced too early.
    
    \item Gradual CTC Ramp-up Phase (epochs 11--30): Linear gradual increase of CTC weight from zero to full weight (Table~\ref{table_optimal_weights}). This smooth integration allows generator to adapt to HTR gradients without disrupting established adversarial training stability.
    
    \item Full Training Phase (epochs 31--50): Training with all loss components active at optimal weights, accompanied by adaptive balancing to maintain dynamic equilibrium between visual and textual losses.
\end{enumerate}

Alternating adversarial training procedure with dual-modal supervision is applied:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Discriminator step: Update $D$ to maximize ability to distinguish real pairs $(I_{\text{gt}}, \text{text}_{\text{gt}})$ from fake pairs $(I_{\text{gen}}, \text{text}_{\text{pred}})$ with simultaneous supervision on visual and textual domains.
    \item Generator step: Update $G$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}$ (combination of five loss components), producing images that simultaneously deceive discriminator and optimize HTR readability.
\end{enumerate}
Updates are performed with balanced 1:1 ratio, ensuring adversarial training stability.

Training configuration is based on empirical tuning for GAN stability and optimal convergence:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Optimization: Generator uses Adam algorithm with momentum parameters adjusted for GAN stability, while discriminator uses SGD with momentum to prevent mode collapse and ensure stable convergence. Both models apply gradient clipping to prevent gradient explosion.
    
    \item Learning rate: Both models use same learning rate with cosine annealing scheduling for smooth convergence without sharp drops.
    
    \item Batch size and dimensions: Small batch size with wide image dimensions preserving fine stroke details on single-line paleographic text.
    
    \item Early stopping: Applied with patience mechanism considering curriculum phases, restoring best weights based on combined PSNR-CER metric with weighting prioritizing visual quality.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Setup}

This section describes the datasets, baseline methods, evaluation metrics, implementation details, and hyperparameter optimization procedures used in the experiments.

\subsection{Datasets}

The proposed method is evaluated on a realistic semi-synthetic dataset constructed from authentic historical documents and qualitative validation on real manuscripts with natural degradation.

\subsubsection{Realistic Semi-Synthetic Dataset for Quantitative Evaluation}

Methodological Rationale: Quantitative evaluation of document restoration requires degraded-clean image pairs with perfect pixel-level alignment to accurately measure PSNR, SSIM, and CER metrics. Real historical documents with natural degradation lack clean ground truth as they cannot be restored to original condition. Therefore, a realistic semi-synthetic dataset was constructed by combining three authentic components~\cite{souibgui2021enhance,zhao2019document}:

Component 1 - Authentic Paleographic Handwriting (REAL):

Authentic 16th--18th century ANRI archival documents that are relatively clean (minimal visual degradation) are used as handwriting source. Documents annotated in Transkribus PAGE XML format were binarized using pretrained DE-GAN model~\cite{souibgui2020degan}, and individual text lines extracted using baseline coordinates with vertical padding, normalized to $128 \times 1024$ pixel dimensions. The extraction result is text line images with authentic paleographic characteristics\textemdash stroke width variation, complex ligatures, individual historical writer styles\textemdash that cannot be replicated by synthetic font rendering or generative models.

Component 2 - Authentic Historical Degradation Patterns (REAL):
Separate ANRI documents experiencing severe natural degradation (paper aging, organic stains, foxing, faded ink, iron gall ink corrosion) are used as degradation background texture sources. Background patches sized $128 \times 1024$ pixels are extracted from non-text regions of these damaged documents, preserving authentic historical degradation patterns formed over centuries of archival storage.

Component 3 - Controlled Degradation Augmentation (SEMI-SYNTHETIC):
To increase variation and create degraded-clean pairs with perfect pixel alignment, a layered degradation pipeline with six computational stages is stochastically applied to the combination of authentic text and real degradation backgrounds:

1. Texture Background Overlay:
Clean text image is composited onto real document texture patch extracted from damaged ANRI collection. Text regions are dimmed to simulate ink absorption into aged paper:
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{degradation}} = I_{\text{bg}} \cdot (1 - M_{\text{text}}) + (\alpha \cdot I_{\text{bg}}) \cdot M_{\text{text}}
\end{equation}
where $M_{\text{text}}$ is normalized text mask and $\alpha$ is dimming factor.

2. Bleed-Through Effect:
Simulates ink penetration from document reverse side by horizontally flipping random text image, applying Gaussian blur, and subtracting from background.

3. Perlin Noise-Based Organic Stains:
Generates natural-looking stain patterns using 3D Perlin noise~\cite{perlin1985image} with varied parameters to create smooth organic stain patterns.

4. Foxing Spots - Age Discoloration:
Simulates age-related discoloration by adding small brownish spots with random intensity.

5. Physical Damage:
Simulates document tears and folds through random lines and darkened patches.

6. Random Blur:
Applies Gaussian blur (smooth degradation), median blur (noise removal), or motion blur (scanner artifacts) with equal probability.

These effects are applied sequentially with randomized combinations, producing diverse and realistic degraded documents while maintaining perfect pixel-level ground truth alignment, eliminating manual annotation errors inherent in real document datasets.

Realistic Semi-Synthetic Dataset Statistics: The dataset consists of 4,739 degraded-clean image pairs ($128 \times 1024$ pixels, grayscale, normalized [0,1]) constructed from authentic ANRI documents. Each pair combines: (1) real historical paleographic handwriting, (2) degradation patterns from authentic naturally damaged archives, and (3) controlled augmentation for variation. This dataset preserves authentic historical document characteristics while providing paired ground truth necessary for high-precision quantitative evaluation.

For generalization evaluation on real historical degradation, a representative document subset from National Archives of the Republic of Indonesia (ANRI) is used as qualitative evaluation set:

\paragraph{Test Dataset Characteristics (n=15)}
Differences from Semi-Synthetic Dataset: This real ANRI dataset fundamentally differs from the semi-synthetic dataset for quantitative evaluation. These documents experienced authentic historical degradation during centuries of archival storage, displaying complex damage patterns that cannot be fully replicated through computational augmentation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Temporal degradation: Natural paper aging with heterogeneous yellowing and local brittleness
    \item Organic artifacts: Biological stains, foxing spots with natural distribution patterns, non-uniform ink degradation
    \item Structural damage: Physical tears, permanent folds, material loss at document edges
    \item Writing variation: Dutch paleographic cursive handwriting with complex ligatures, period abbreviations, and writing style inconsistencies
\end{itemize}

Real ANRI documents lack paired visual ground truth (clean images) because authentic historical documents cannot be restored to original condition\textemdash physical and chemical damage over centuries is irreversible. Therefore, evaluation on real dataset is qualitative through visual inspection and paleography expert validation, complementing quantitative evaluation on semi-synthetic dataset. This dual evaluation approach follows standard practice in historical document restoration research~\cite{souibgui2021enhance,souibgui2020degan}: realistic semi-synthetic dataset (constructed from authentic documents with controlled augmentation) for precision quantitative measurement, and real dataset with natural degradation for qualitative generalization validation on real-world conditions.

\subsection{Baseline Methods and Evaluation Strategy}

Evaluation of the proposed method is conducted through two complementary approaches: (1) quantitative comparison with classical methods that can be replicated under identical experimental conditions, and (2) systematic ablation study to validate contribution of each architectural component.

\subsubsection{Classical Methods for Quantitative Comparison}

Classical methods are selected as quantitative baselines because they can be re-implemented on identical dataset without requiring retraining:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item No Enhancement (Degraded Input Baseline): Direct HTR on degraded images to measure degradation impact on HTR performance
    \item Otsu Thresholding~\cite{otsu1979threshold}: Histogram-based global binarization for document cleaning
    \item Sauvola Adaptive Thresholding~\cite{sauvola2000adaptive}: Local adaptive binarization for documents with non-uniform illumination
\end{enumerate}

Classical methods use standard OpenCV implementation with parameters optimized through grid search on validation subset.

\subsubsection{State-of-the-Art Deep Learning Methods (Research Context)}

Deep learning-based methods discussed in literature review (Section II) serve as research context to position the proposed contribution:

\begin{itemize}
    \item U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet}: Encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections for image restoration
    \item DE-GAN~\cite{souibgui2020degan}: Conditional GAN for document enhancement with visual quality focus
    \item HTR-GAN~\cite{souibgui2021enhance}: GAN with jointly trained recognizer for HTR-oriented restoration
    \item DocEnTr: Transformer-based architecture with self-attention mechanism for document enhancement
\end{itemize}

Direct quantitative comparison with state-of-the-art deep learning methods is not performed because: (1) these methods are trained on different datasets with non-identical degradation characteristics, making direct comparison unfair~\cite{tensmeyer2017document}, (2) official reproducible implementations are not available for most methods, and (3) differences in architecture, training protocols, and loss functions make isolating specific contributions very difficult. This approach follows common evaluation practice in document restoration research where comparisons are performed only under identically replicable experimental conditions.

\subsubsection{Ablation Study as Component Validation}

To systematically validate contribution of each architectural component, incremental ablation study is performed by adding loss components progressively (Section V-B):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Experiment 01 (Pixel Only): Equivalent to standard U-Net for image restoration
    \item Experiment 02 (+Adversarial): Equivalent to standard GAN without HTR component
    \item Experiment 03 (+Perceptual): Enhancement with VGG-based perceptual loss
    \item Experiment 04 (+CTC): Optimal configuration with HTR integration
    \item Experiment 05 (+Recognizer Features): Complete configuration for component redundancy validation
\end{enumerate}

This ablation approach provides comprehensive understanding of relative contribution of each component and empirically validates architectural design decisions. Ablation results (Table~\ref{table_ablation_loss}) show that 4-component configuration (Experiment 04) achieves optimal performance with best trade-off between visual quality and HTR readability.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Visual quality and text recognition metrics are used for evaluation.

\subsubsection{Visual Quality Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR): Measures pixel-level similarity
    \begin{equation}
    \text{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\text{MAX}^2}{\text{MSE}}\right)
    \end{equation}
    where MAX=1.0 for images normalized to range [0,1], and MSE is Mean Squared Error.

    \item Structural Similarity Index (SSIM): Assesses perceived quality by considering luminance, contrast, and structure~\cite{wang2004ssim}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{SSIM}(x,y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + c_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + c_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + c_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + c_2)}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Artifact Detection Metrics (Secondary Monitoring)}

To monitor generation quality during training and ensure generator does not produce visual artifacts that interfere with text readability, three additional metrics are used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Noise Variance}: Variance of Laplacian operator to measure high-frequency noise
    
    \item \textit{Isolated White Ratio}: Ratio of isolated white pixels to total pixels, detecting salt noise artifacts
    
    \item \textit{Local Variance}: Variance of local deviations from smoothed image, measuring restoration result smoothness
\end{itemize}

These metrics are used for training monitoring and quality validation, but not reported as primary evaluation metrics as they are diagnostic in nature.

\subsubsection{HTR Performance Metrics}

A hybrid CNN-Transformer model adapted on historical documents is used to evaluate HTR performance. This model is frozen during evaluation to ensure measurement consistency:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Character Error Rate} (CER): Percentage of character-level errors, calculated using Levenshtein edit distance:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{CER} = \frac{\text{EditDistance}(\text{prediction}, \text{reference})}{\text{length}(\text{reference})}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textit{Word Error Rate} (WER): Percentage of word-level errors, calculated with edit distance at word level:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{WER} = \frac{\text{EditDistance}(\text{predicted words}, \text{reference words})}{\text{length}(\text{reference words})}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Frozen Recognizer CER Baseline Clarification} To avoid confusion, this paper uses three different frozen recognizer CER baseline values:\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt]
    \item CER\textsubscript{pretrain} = 33.72\%: Performance of hybrid CNN-Transformer recognizer (CNN encoder with 6-layer transformer and CTC decoder) when evaluated on separate ANRI validation dataset ($n$=948) for initial generalization validation. This value is used to verify that recognizer can handle Dutch paleographic handwriting.
    \item CER\textsubscript{val-GT} = 26.57\%: Frozen recognizer performance on clean ground truth images from this study's validation set ($n$=710). This value represents theoretical upper bound for validation set.
    \item CER\textsubscript{test-GT} = 34.1\%: Frozen recognizer performance on clean ground truth images from this study's test set ($n$=712). This value represents theoretical upper bound for test set and serves as primary reference for assessing restoration quality.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.5em}
Difference between CER\textsubscript{val-GT} (26.57\%) vs CER\textsubscript{test-GT} (34.1\%), namely $\Delta$=7.53 percentage points, reflects that test set contains samples with higher paleographic complexity (more complex ligatures, greater handwriting style variation). This difference pattern is consistent with restoration results: CER\textsubscript{val-restored} = 27.11\% vs CER\textsubscript{test-restored} = 34.9\% ($\Delta$=7.79\%), confirming that gap is not caused by overfitting but rather dataset characteristic differences.

Root Cause Analysis: In-depth analysis identifies five factors explaining these findings:

(1) Generator Bottleneck: Both experiments use identical generator architecture (enhanced U-Net). Output quality limit is determined by generator capacity, not discriminator feedback complexity. Generator with limited capacity cannot produce substantially better outputs even though discriminator provides more sophisticated signals.

(2) Prediction Text Mode Limitations: Training configuration uses prediction text mode, meaning text input to dual-modal discriminator comes from generator output (CER 27.11\%), not ground truth. LSTM branch receives text with 27\% error rate, so it cannot learn from accurate text patterns. Cross-modal attention mechanism is limited by noisy prediction text quality.

(3) Loss Function Dominance: Loss contribution analysis shows that adversarial loss only contributes small portion to total training signal. Discriminator architecture difference only affects minority component of total gradient.

(4) Frozen Recognizer Constraints: HTR recognizer is frozen with CER\textsubscript{val-GT} = 26.57\% on clean validation set images. Generated images achieve CER\textsubscript{val-restored} = 27.11\%, only 0.54 percentage points from theoretical upper bound. Very limited improvement space regardless of discriminator architecture.

(5) Near-Optimal Performance: Model already reached performance plateau. Marginal improvements expected from architectural modifications without paradigm shift in training protocol.

Scientific Interpretation: This finding is not dual-modal architecture failure, but rather valuable scientific insight showing that in GAN-HTR framework: (a) training protocol design (text supervision mode, loss weight balance) has more significant impact than discriminator architectural complexity, (b) generator capacity is main bottleneck for output quality improvement, (c) prediction text mode limits dual-modal effectiveness because LSTM learns from noisy text, not ground truth.

Ablation Study Validity: Although only comparing two variants (single CNN vs dual-modal), this study remains valid for primary purpose: validating whether adding text branch and cross-attention mechanism provides significant improvement. Results show that in current configuration, dual-modal architectural contribution is marginal. Further studies with dual-modal variants without cross-attention or with ground truth text supervision can provide additional insights about which components are most influential.

Methodological Note: Ablation evaluation performed on validation set (n=710) used for model selection (epoch 44 checkpoint), following standard ablation study practice where components are evaluated on development dataset for rapid iteration and preventing test set contamination. This approach is consistent with model selection protocol where epoch 44 checkpoint is optimized based on combined PSNR-CER criteria on same validation set. Test set (n=712) is reserved for final generalization evaluation reported in Table~\ref{table_synthetic_results}. Metric differences between validation (PSNR 30.91 dB, CER 27.11\%) and test (PSNR 30.74 dB, CER 34.9\%) reflect natural variation of different samples, not overfitting, and confirm that ablation findings remain valid for generalization.

Implications for Future Research: To maximize dual-modal architecture benefits, modifications required: (1) ground truth text supervision, (2) adversarial weight increase to strengthen discriminator influence, (3) text-aware generator architecture with integrated text encoder, or (4) progressive training strategy (phase 1: single CNN focuses on visual, phase 2: dual-modal refines text readability).

\subsubsection{In-Depth Analysis: Recognizer Feature Loss Ineffectiveness}
\label{sec:recfeat_analysis}

Although recognizer feature loss is conceptually promising for enhancing alignment between restored images and HTR recognizer internal representations, ablation study results show that adding this component actually degrades HTR performance (CER increases from 29.66\% to 30.16\%, degradation +0.50\%). In-depth investigation reveals four interconnected fundamental factors.

\textit{Feature-level misalignment.} Recognizer feature loss extracts representations from intermediate layers in the HTR recognizer architecture, located before Transformer processing. This extraction position creates fundamental misalignment between optimization objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Mid-level features: Represent convolutional architectural patterns (edges, character components) optimized for Transformer input preparation, not for human visual perception or high-level semantic quality.
    
    \item Document restoration needs: Require optimization on high-level semantic quality (text clarity, readability) and visual aesthetics (smooth background, natural texture, artifact suppression).
    
    \item Representation-level conflict: Mean squared loss on mid-level features does not align with either high-level perceptual quality metrics or pixel-level reconstruction.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Loss magnitude imbalance.} Training log analysis at epoch 15 reveals extreme magnitude disparity across loss components:

    \item Gradient flow: CTC and perceptual components dominate with $\approx$100-200$\times$ greater strength, effectively masking recognizer feature gradients in optimization.
\end{itemize}

Implication: Recognizer feature has negligible influence on generator gradient updates, thus cannot provide meaningful guidance for optimization trajectory.

\textit{Gradient interference.} Perceptual loss and recognizer feature loss optimize against different feature spaces with potentially conflicting objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Perceptual loss: Pushes generator toward smooth aesthetics aligned with human perception (gradients toward natural texture, smooth edges, artifact suppression).
    
    \item Recognizer feature loss: Pushes generator to match sharp character patterns optimized for recognition (gradients toward enhanced edge sharpness for Transformer processing).
\end{itemize}

Empirical result: Experiment 5 (with recognizer feature) achieves CER 30.16\% compared to Experiment 4 (without recognizer feature) with CER 29.66\%, indicating that opposing gradients disrupt optimal convergence for HTR performance. Although Experiment 5 adds an additional loss component, the CER increase shows recognizer feature optimizes objectives misaligned with downstream HTR performance.

\textit{Computational burden without benefit.} Using recognizer feature loss requires recognizer architecture modification to produce dual outputs, increasing memory footprint approximately 15--20\% and training time 5--10\%.

Cost-benefit analysis shows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cost: 15--20\% memory increase, 5--10\% training time increase
    \item Benefit: CER degradation +0.50\% (from 29.66\% to 30.16\%), marginal PSNR decrease (-0.01 dB), negligible SSIM increase (+0.0002)
    \item Conclusion: Negative cost-benefit ratio, computational burden provides no meaningful performance improvement
\end{itemize}

Conclusion: In-depth analysis reveals that recognizer feature loss is fundamentally misaligned with document restoration objectives due to feature-level misalignment, negligible magnitude (0.45\% of total loss), potential gradient interference, and computational overhead without benefit.

Recommendation: Experiment 4 configuration (Pixel + Adversarial + Perceptual + CTC) without recognizer feature loss achieves optimal performance. CTC loss already suffices to provide text-awareness signal to the generator without requiring additional feature alignment.

Scientific contribution: This finding demonstrates that feature-level selection is highly influential in multi-objective optimization for document restoration. Auxiliary task features must semantically align with primary task objectives, not merely be conceptually related.

Frozen Recognizer Strategy Selection Justification:

1. Computational efficiency: Pre-trained recognizer (27.86M parameters) requires no gradient computation, so only the generator (89.4M parameters) and discriminator (18.9M parameters) are optimized. This approach reduces optimization complexity and prevents gradient conflicts between components. Actual recorded training time is 48 GPU-hours for 50 epochs with batch size 2 on RTX A4000 16GB GPU, more efficient compared to joint training strategy requiring simultaneous optimization of all components.

2. Training stability: Frozen recognizer prevents conflicting gradients between reconstruction objectives ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}$) and recognition objectives ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{ctc}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec-feat}}$). Literature shows joint training on multi-task GANs is prone to mode collapse and oscillating convergence~\cite{souibgui2022,kang2021pay}.

3. Performance preservation: Hybrid CNN-Transformer recognizer (6-layer transformer encoder with CTC decoder) trained on ANRI dataset achieves CER 33.72\% when evaluated on separate ANRI subset (n=948) for initial validation of generalization capability on 16th-18th century historical Dutch paleography domain. Freezing the recognizer maintains this generalization capability and prevents catastrophic forgetting~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} that can occur if the recognizer is retrained on distribution shift from enhanced images. The proposed framework achieves CER 27.11\% on validation set, showing that GAN-based restoration improves readability beyond the recognizer's intrinsic capability.

4. Differentiation from literature: Unlike Souibgui \textit{et al.}~\cite{souibgui2022} who use joint recognizer-generator training (potentially gradient instability), this research freezes the pre-trained recognizer for optimization stability. This strategy prevents documented gradient conflicts in multi-task GANs~\cite{souibgui2022,kang2021pay}, allowing the generator to focus on visual restoration without degrading the recognizer.

\section{Discussion}

This section discusses key findings, theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and future research directions.

\subsection{Dual-Modal Architecture Contribution and Training Protocol Insights}

The discriminator ablation study (Table~\ref{table_ablation_discriminator}) reveals important findings about HTR-oriented GAN training dynamics. Although the dual-modal architecture is theoretically more powerful, empirical results show minimal improvement compared to single CNN discriminator ($\Delta$PSNR +0.28 dB, $\Delta$CER -0.01\%, not statistically significant with $p > 0.05$). 

In-depth analysis identifies that the marginal contribution of dual-modal architecture in this research is primarily due to implementation limitations, not inherent architectural limitations. Three key factors from research design choices that limit dual-modal effectiveness:

\textit{Prediction text mode:} This research implementation uses predicted text as discriminator input, so the LSTM branch receives text from generator output (CER 27.11\%) rather than ground truth text. Noisy text input (27\% error rate) limits LSTM's ability to learn accurate text patterns, and the cross-modal attention mechanism cannot fully exploit clean text semantics. With modification using ground truth text supervision, dual-modal architecture potential can be better realized.

\textit{Loss weight imbalance:} Adversarial loss contribution is only about 5\% of total training signal (weight 3.0 compared to pixel loss 50.0 and recognition feature 8.0). Discriminator architecture changes only affect the minority component, while 95\% of gradients come from identical loss components for both experiments. To amplify discriminator influence, adversarial weight needs to be increased (e.g., from 3.0 to 10.0).

\textit{Generator capacity bottleneck:} Both experiments use identical generator. Output quality ceiling is determined by generator architecture, not discriminator feedback sophistication. Generator with limited capacity cannot fully exploit more complex discriminator.

The dual-modal architecture remains theoretically valuable because it can: (a) explicitly model sequential text coherence through LSTM, (b) detect subtle character connectivity issues, (c) provide multi-modal evaluation signal. To realize full potential in future research, protocol modifications are required: ground truth text supervision, higher adversarial weight (e.g., from 3.0 to 10.0), or text-aware generator architecture.

This finding provides insight that in multi-objective GAN optimization, architectural innovation must be accompanied by aligned training protocol to achieve desired benefits. The marginal dual-modal contribution in this research reflects implementation misalignment, not conceptual architectural failure. Discriminator complexity alone does not guarantee improvement if training configuration is not designed to exploit it optimally.

Qualitative analysis on successful cases reveals that dual-modal discriminator helps maintain character connectivity and stroke continuity in cursive handwriting. However, for challenging degradations, both discriminator variants show comparable performance, confirming that generator capacity is the primary limiting factor.

\subsection{Frozen Recognizer Strategy and Training Stability}

The selection of frozen pre-trained recognizer as replacement for joint training (as proposed in previous research~\cite{souibgui2021enhance}) proves to provide significant benefits. Joint training introduces fundamental optimization challenges: recognizer and generator can evolve in conflicting directions, and recognizer degradation during training produces unstable gradients.

The frozen recognizer approach provides consistent and reliable text-aware feedback during training. By training the recognizer beforehand on diverse clean handwriting dataset, strong text feature representations have been encoded and can guide restoration without requiring text labels during GAN training. This stability is confirmed through smooth convergence trajectory without significant oscillations (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence_analysis}).

\subsection{Loss Weight Optimization Insights}

Grid search on hyperparameters reveals that adversarial loss weight and perceptual loss are the most influential hyperparameters for thin stroke preservation. This finding aligns with GAN theory: adversarial loss regulates the trade-off between realism and fidelity, while perceptual loss maintains stroke topology through VGG features.

Excessively high adversarial weight causes mode collapse (generator produces limited variation) and training instability. Conversely, excessively low weight produces blurry unrealistic output. The optimal value found (3.0) balances both concerns while maintaining training stability.

Recognition feature loss weight has moderate impact, showing that text-aware guidance provides added value but must be balanced with visual quality objectives. Ablation study (Section V-B) confirms that 4-component configuration without recognition feature loss achieves optimal balance between visual quality and HTR readability.

\subsection{Generalization to Real Historical Documents}

Qualitative evaluation on real ANRI documents (Section V-C) shows reasonable generalization despite the model being trained only on synthetic degradation, with the majority of documents showing useful improvement. Paleography expert validation indicates practicality of the proposed method for deployment in real-world archival workflows.

However, domain gap effects are observed in extreme degradation cases (13.3\% failure rate), particularly for heavy iron gall ink corrosion and severely faded ink. This is understandable because synthetic degradation pipeline does not yet cover chemical corrosion models and extreme severity levels found in 250--450 year old documents.

Strategies with potential to improve generalization:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Domain adaptation through fine-tuning on small set of real degraded documents with few-shot learning strategy
    \item Synthetic degradation enhancement by incorporating more realistic aging patterns and iron gall ink chemical corrosion models
    \item Unsupervised learning to leverage unlabeled real documents through self-supervised learning techniques
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Deployment Considerations}

To deploy the system in archival digitization workflows, several practical considerations need attention:

The proposed model has higher computational complexity compared to traditional binarization methods (Sauvola, Otsu) due to GAN architecture with dual-modal discriminator and frozen recognizer. Training time recorded 48 GPU-hours for 50 epochs on RTX A4000 16GB. For production deployment, inference can be performed with batch processing to improve throughput, suitable for large-scale archival projects with thousands of documents. The improvement in HTR accuracy (detailed in Section~V) can justify computational investment for high-value archival digitization applications. Although average performance is strong, failure cases remain, especially for complex paleographic ligatures and severely faded text, so human review is recommended for important documents, or automated confidence assessment mechanisms can be deployed to flag restorations requiring manual verification.

Document restoration modifies original appearance. For scientific use and archival preservation, original and restored versions must be archived separately with clear provenance metadata, including model information used, restoration date, and confidence scores.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research Directions}

Current Research Limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Trade-off Quantitative Evaluation vs. Full Realism: Quantitative evaluation requires paired ground truth unavailable for real historical documents. The realistic semi-synthetic dataset constructed from authentic ANRI documents provides real paleographic handwriting and authentic historical degradation patterns, but controlled augmentation may not fully replicate centuries-old chemical degradation complexity (iron gall ink corrosion, local pH changes). Qualitative validation results on 15 ANRI manuscripts with natural degradation show effective generalization, confirming practical relevance despite quantitative evaluation using augmented dataset.
    \item Cross-Domain Generalization Limitations: Trained primarily on Latin script (Dutch); cross-language and cross-script generalization not comprehensively tested
    \item Extreme Degradation and Low Contrast: Model struggles with extreme cases (contrast $<$30, very heavy fading) underrepresented in training dataset distribution, as validated in real ANRI document evaluation
    \item Specific Failure Patterns: (a) Complex paleographic ligatures (62.9\% of CER$>$15\% cases), (b) Overlapping text with archival stamps, (c) Numeric and special symbol artifacts (ƒ, §, =), (d) Iron gall ink corrosion with complex chemical effects
\end{enumerate}

Future Research Directions:

Architecture and Training Protocol Improvements:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Alternative feature space exploration for HTR guidance: post-transformer semantic features, multi-scale feature matching with adaptive weights, cosine similarity replacing MSE, and attention-guided feature alignment
    \item Training protocol optimization for dual-modal effectiveness: ground truth text supervision, dynamic loss weight scheduling, text-aware generator architecture, and progressive training strategy
    \item Generator architecture enhancement with transformer-based models or diffusion models for higher representation capacity
\end{enumerate}

Generalization and Evaluation Improvements:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{8}
    \item Uncertainty quantification with confidence scores for restoration quality
    \item Cross-language and cross-script evaluation (Arabic, Malay, English, Indonesian and other historical scripts)
    \item \textit{Few-shot} adaptation to enable rapid adjustment to new document types with minimal examples
    \item Real historical document ground truth creation through collaboration with paleography experts
\end{enumerate}

Practical Deployment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{12}
    \item Interactive restoration with user feedback for challenging regions
    \item Systematic loss weight optimization using Bayesian optimization or evolution-based search
    \item Automated quality control system development for hallucination and artifact detection
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ethical Considerations and Historical Integrity Preservation}

\subsubsection{Ethics of Historical Document Modification}

Automated restoration of historical documents raises important ethical questions that must be considered in practical deployment. Restored documents using the proposed method cannot replace original documents and must be treated as research aids, not as absolute historical truth.

Although the current research implementation produces restored images without automatic metadata, the recommended production system for archival deployment should include comprehensive metadata encompassing: labels indicating artificial intelligence usage, model version used, restoration date, estimated restoration quality confidence, and links to original documents for verification. Archival institutions are recommended to implement dual archival protocols including: (1) storage of original documents as master copies, (2) storage of restored versions as derivative copies with clear labels, (3) use of lossless formats (TIFF or PNG), (4) implementation of cryptographic checksums for integrity verification, and (5) maintenance of audit records of all processing steps.

\subsubsection{Hallucination Detection and Mitigation}

Generative models in general have the potential to add information not present in the original document, especially in severely degraded regions. The proposed method is designed with a conservative restoration strategy prioritizing false negatives (missed enhancement opportunities) over false positives (hallucinated strokes), as validated through qualitative analysis of ANRI documents. Although this design minimizes hallucination risk, future research can develop explicit detection mechanisms for production systems, such as: (1) stroke density analysis by comparing the number of text pixels before and after restoration to detect abnormal stroke additions, (2) uncertainty quantification to flag low-confidence regions as requiring manual verification, and (3) consistency checking by comparing results from multiple inferences to ensure stable outputs.

Such automated detection systems can be developed to send manual verification alerts if significant image modifications exceed defined thresholds, ensuring that potential hallucinations can be identified before documents are used for historical analysis. Development of such automated quality control systems is a future research direction.

\subsubsection{Training Data Bias}

The synthetic dataset used for GAN training (derived from relatively clean ANRI documents with multi-stage synthetic degradation pipelines) does not fully replicate natural degradation of VOC-era documents (1650--1800) due to characteristic differences: (1) synthetic degradation (bleed-through, texture noise, blur) does not capture the full complexity of historical degradation such as iron gall ink corrosion with local pH changes, (2) synthetic aging patterns do not fully replicate natural degradation over centuries, and (3) archival storage condition variations (humidity, temperature, light exposure) affecting real document degradation.

To mitigate this bias, future research can explore several strategies: fine-tuning on real degraded ANRI documents for domain adaptation, few-shot learning with minimal examples from target domain, and corrective feedback integration involving paleography experts in the loop for challenging cases.

\subsubsection{Recommendations for Practitioners}

For archival managers, it is recommended to: (1) use restored images as starting points for analysis, not as final truth, (2) always verify critical results by comparing with original documents, (3) document model information (version, configuration, restoration date) in archival records, and (4) implement dual archival protocols to maintain original document integrity. The current limitation is that the system does not yet provide automatic confidence scores, so quality assessment requires visual inspection by experts.

For system developers who will implement this method in production, it is recommended to: (1) implement explainability tools such as attention maps for restoration process transparency, (2) develop uncertainty estimation for each restoration prediction, (3) maintain model versioning systems for reproducibility, (4) conduct periodic bias \textit{audits} to ensure fairness and accuracy, and (5) integrate hallucination detection mechanisms for quality control.

\section{Conclusion}

This research proposes an HTR-oriented GAN-based document restoration framework that addresses limitations of conventional methods in optimizing text readability for automatic recognition systems. The proposed method integrates two main contributions: (1) a \textit{frozen HTR recognizer} strategy providing stable text-aware gradients without joint training instability, proven to be a key success factor of the system through ablation study ($p<0.001$), and (2) multi-component loss function optimization identifying the 4-component configuration (pixel, adversarial, perceptual, CTC) as the optimal balance between visual quality and HTR readability. Exploration of dual-modal discriminator architecture shows statistically insignificant marginal contribution ($\Delta$PSNR +0.28~dB, $p>0.05$), confirming that training protocol and frozen recognizer strategy are more dominant than discriminator architecture complexity.

Quantitative evaluation on 712 test images from a realistic semi-synthetic dataset based on 16th-18th century ANRI paleographic documents demonstrates CER reduction from 83.4\% (degraded condition) to 34.9\% (58.2\% relative reduction, $p<0.001$), approaching theoretical performance on clean images (CER 34.1\%), while maintaining high visual quality (PSNR 30.74~dB, SSIM 0.987). Qualitative validation on 15 authentic ANRI manuscripts confirms effective generalization to real historical degradation that cannot be synthetically replicated. Systematic ablation study validates that the 4-component loss combination achieves optimality in multi-objective space, with the important finding that recognizer feature loss is counterproductive and should be avoided.

The main finding of this research is that explicit integration of HTR objectives through frozen recognizer during GAN training produces outputs optimized for machine readability while maintaining visual quality, with practical implications for large-scale historical archival digitization projects. Limitations include challenges with extreme degradation (contrast $<$30, very heavy fading), complex paleographic ligatures (62.9\% of failure cases), and restriction to Latin paleographic script. Future research will focus on paleographic ligature preservation, historical chemical degradation modeling (iron gall ink corrosion), layered document separation, low-contrast robustness improvement, and transfer-learning validation on multi-language and multi-script corpora to confirm cross-domain generalization.
